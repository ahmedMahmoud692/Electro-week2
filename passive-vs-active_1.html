Engineers Hub · Electricity Module

Passive components store and shape.
Active components inject and control.

Every circuit is built from two families of parts. One family only ever gives back energy it was handed — resistors, capacitors, inductors. The other pulls extra energy from a power supply and uses it to amplify or switch a signal — transistors, diodes, ICs. Everything else in circuit design follows from that one difference.

SIGNAL PATH — dims through R1 / C1 (passive, energy only leaves), brightens through Q1 / U1 (active, energy is added from an external supply)
01

The core distinction

Passive components

They can only store, release, or resist energy already in the circuit

A passive component never adds net energy to a signal and can't produce gain. It works with zero external power source of its own — whatever current or voltage arrives is all it ever has to work with. Resistors dissipate it as heat, capacitors and inductors store it briefly and hand it back.

Quick test → Unplug every power supply from the circuit. If the part still behaves exactly the same way (just resisting, storing, or filtering), it's passive.
Active components

They draw on an external power source to control or amplify a signal

An active component needs its own supply rail to function. It uses a small input signal to control a much larger flow of energy from that supply — that's what makes amplification and switching possible. Take away the external power and the component stops doing its job entirely, even if a signal is still present.

Quick test → Unplug every power supply from the circuit. If the part goes dead or stops controlling anything, it's active.
02

Passive components, with examples

Resistor

Limits current, drops voltage

Converts electrical energy to heat in direct proportion to current (Ohm's law). No memory, no storage — instantaneous.

Applications: LED circuits, sensors, control systems, and electronic devices in general.
Capacitor

Stores charge in an electric field

Blocks DC once charged, passes AC. Used to smooth, filter, or bridge brief gaps in supply voltage.

Applications: phone chargers, computer motherboards, power supplies, and communication systems.
Inductor

Stores energy in a magnetic field

Resists sudden changes in current. Combined with a capacitor, forms the basis of most filters and power converters.

Applications: power converters, motors, transformers, and renewable energy systems.
Transformer

Transfers energy between coils

Steps voltage up or down through magnetic coupling — no electrical connection between windings, no gain.

Applications: power grids, electrical substations, chargers, and industrial systems.
03

Active components, with examples

Diode

Allows current to flow in one direction only

Acts as a one-way valve for electricity — conducts when forward-biased, blocks when reverse-biased. That nonlinear, threshold-based behavior sets it apart from purely linear passive parts.

Applications: chargers, power supplies, solar energy systems, and protection circuits.
LED (Light Emitting Diode)

Converts electrical energy into light

A special diode that emits photons when current flows through it in the forward direction — same one-way behavior as a standard diode, with light as the output.

Applications: displays, lighting systems, indicators, and automotive lighting.
Transistor

Amplifies signals and switches current

A tiny base/gate current or voltage governs a much larger current drawn from the supply. Modern civilization runs on this idea — smartphones, computers, the internet, and AI hardware all depend on transistors.

Applications: signal amplification, digital logic, and current switching in virtually every electronic device.
MOSFET

Efficient electrical switching and power control

A type of transistor optimized for switching larger currents with very low power loss, controlled by a gate voltage rather than a continuous current — key to modern power electronics.

Applications: electric vehicles, GPUs, CPUs, power electronics, and renewable energy systems.
